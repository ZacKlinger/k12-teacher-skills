# Lesson design — the structure that never changes

Read this before drafting any lesson. Everything here applies to both the packet and the deck.

## The five fixed parts

Every session, 60 or 90 minutes, has these in this order. Nothing else is guaranteed.

1. **Do Now** (3-6 min) — on the board when students walk in, answerable with no instruction, and
   connected to today. Retrieval from yesterday, a prediction, a noticing about an image or a
   number. It has a sentence stem. It is never busywork and it is never new content.
2. **Objective + Agenda** (2-3 min) — one "I can…" sentence in student language, then the agenda
   with times. Students should be able to see, at any moment, where they are in the period. The
   agenda lives on the deck and the board — not on the packet, where it costs a third of page 1 and
   gets read off the screen anyway.
3. **The body** — see block sizing below.
4. **Closing** (6-10 min) — students answer something that shows whether the objective landed.
   Written, one prompt, with a stem. Collected or checked before they leave. Not a summary by the
   teacher, and never cut for time — it is scheduled with the transition inside it.
   The closing question aims at **the difficulty the point of view named** (SKILL.md Step 2), which
   is the hardest case of the standard, not its easiest version. A closing that most students pass
   whether or not the lesson landed tells you nothing about tomorrow. It uses only ideas, terms and
   representations students already worked with earlier in the period; nothing appears there for
   the first time. Under the closing sits one short reflection line ("Which part was hardest? What
   made it hard?") so every packet has at least one prompt that asks a student about their own
   thinking. On science days the closing is a short claim-evidence-reasoning, see "Science days".
5. **Exit path** — what a student who finishes early does, and what a student who didn't finish
   does. One line each in the lesson plan.

## Block sizing

Minutes must sum exactly to the period, transitions included inside blocks, never as buffer.
No single block exceeds 20 minutes. If a task needs more, split it with a check-in between.

**Math, 60 minutes:** Do Now 5 · Objective + Agenda 3 · Model 10-12 · Partner or independent work
18-20 · Discuss 12 · Closing 8.

**Math, 90-minute block:** Do Now 5 · Objective + Agenda 3 · Model 15 · Work block 1 20 ·
Discuss 12 · Media + debrief 10 · Work block 2 15 · Closing 10.

**Science** follows its own order, with the investigation ahead of the explanation. The blocks are
in "Science days" below.

The **Discuss** block belongs to the students: most of its minutes are students talking to each
other about the hardest case, not answering you one at a time. It has no minimum length; what it
must have is a named talk move. See "Student talk".

Nobody in this room sits productively for 90 minutes. On a block day, at least one of the talk
moves should get students out of their seats (the catalog marks which ones do).

## Non-negotiables in every lesson

**Student talk.** Every lesson has **at least two** moments of student-to-student talk, named and
planned, whatever the period's length: one in the main work or Discuss block, and one anywhere else
(the do-now debrief, after the video, rehearsing the closing). A block day usually earns three.
What counts is the move, not the minutes: two minutes of built talk beats a long block of drifting.
Pick each move from the catalog below and name it in both the plan and the deck. The talk carries content: every move has a question worth talking about, a
sentence stem for the first speaker, and a job for the listener. In this room talk has to be
built, not hoped for:

- **Partners are assigned**, from the profile's pairings when it has them. Say who is Partner A.
- **Think time comes first.** Thirty seconds of silent thinking, or a written first line, before
  anyone speaks. A student who has something on paper has something to say.
- **The stem is on the screen** while they talk, the same stem as on the packet.
- **The listener has a job**: say it back, agree or disagree, or add one thing.
- **Rehearse before reporting.** Every student says it to a partner before anyone says it to the
  room. The student who reports out is often reading what their partner helped them build.
- **The timer is visible**, and short. Two to four minutes of paired talk beats ten minutes of
  drifting.

**Sentence stems.** Every task that asks for a composed sentence gets one, placed on that task —
not a bank at the top of the page. A stem helps a student start; it never contains the answer.
Two forms:

- Starter: *"One thing I notice is…"*
- Frame with blanks: *"The ______ got bigger because ______."*

Tasks answered with a number, a circled choice, a word, or a drawing need no stem.

**Differentiation, on the same page.** Everyone works from one packet — pulling out a different
worksheet marks students in front of each other. Differentiate inside the task instead:

- Tier within the question: part (a) everyone does, part (b) pushes further. Label them by what
  they ask, not by level.
- **The hardest case of the standard lives in part (a)**, with its scaffold. Part (b) goes
  further, never harder than the standard. If the only place the real difficulty shows up is the
  extension, most of the room never meets it.
- The first item is worked as an example, right there on the page.
- A word bank or reference strip sits next to the question that needs it, not on a separate page.
- Number choices are smaller in the first item and grow, and the last item everyone does uses the
  standard's real numbers (its decimals, fractions, or range). A student who stalls has still done
  real thinking, and a student who finishes has met the standard as written.
- The lesson plan names which students get which support by need ("students who need the equation
  restated," not by name unless the profile uses names).

**Scaffolding.** Chunk directions to one instruction per line. Bold the verb. Give a worked
example before independent practice, every time. Vocabulary gets defined in the sentence where it
first appears, in words a 3rd-5th grade reader knows. Numbers stay friendly until the concept is
secure, then reach the standard's full range.

**Vocabulary, defined where it lands.** Every new word the lesson needs gets defined on the slide
at the moment it's first used — the word, how to say it, what it means in one short sentence, and a
non-example. Not a vocabulary list at the front of the packet, which is a reading task students do
once and never return to. The same wording appears on the packet page where the word first shows up.

**Media.** Every lesson includes something to look at that isn't text: a verified video, a
photograph, a data graphic, a physical object, a demonstration. Video gets a purpose before it
plays ("watch for the moment the water changes") and a two-minute debrief after. Long videos get
stop-points, not a full play.

## Talk catalog

Every move here is student to student. "Moves" marks the ones that also get students out of their
seats.

| Move | How it runs | Moves | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turn-and-talk | Question on screen, 30 seconds of think time, A talks for one minute, B for one minute | | Any block; the default short move |
| Think-pair-share | Think alone and write one line, pair and compare, a few pairs share with the room | | Opening the Discuss block |
| Partner compare | Partners put their answers side by side, find one difference, and decide together who is right and why | | Checking work; error analysis |
| Say it back | B restates what A said ("You said…"), A confirms or fixes it, then switch | | New vocabulary; explanations |
| Agree / disagree / add on | Each partner responds to a posted claim with one of three stems: "I agree because…", "I disagree because…", "I want to add…" | | Claims, predictions, CER |
| Stand up, hand up, pair up | Students stand, find a partner who isn't their seat partner, talk, then return | ✓ | Energy reset with content |
| Four corners, defend | Students walk to the corner that matches their answer, talk with the others there, then one student per corner defends it to the room | ✓ | Predictions and common wrong answers |
| Gallery walk with a partner | Pairs circulate to posted work or images with one question per stop and a capture sheet | ✓ | Comparing examples, sharing group work |
| Stations in pairs | 3-4 tables, 8-10 minutes each, timer on the screen, partners work and talk together | ✓ | Labs, multi-step practice |
| Human graph, then talk | Students stand on a floor axis to make a class data set, then talk with a neighbor about what the shape shows | ✓ | Data, distributions, fractions and percents |

## Science days

On a science day, students meet the evidence before the explanation. Explicit instruction still
happens, and it stays short and clear, but it comes after the investigation and names what students
found rather than telling them what they are about to find.

**Block sizing, 60 minutes:** Do Now 5 · Objective + Agenda 3 · Phenomenon 7 · Investigate 15 ·
Partner sense-making 10 · Name it + revise the model 10 · Closing (CER) 10.

**Block sizing, 90-minute block:** Do Now 5 · Objective + Agenda 3 · Phenomenon + first model 12 ·
Investigate 20 · Partner sense-making 15 · Name it + media 15 · Revise the model 10 ·
Closing (CER) 10.

- **The phenomenon is something students can see.** A demonstration, a photograph, a short clip, a
  data set from a real event. It is posed as a question students will explain ("Why did the lettuce
  in tray B die?"), never as an illustration of what you are about to teach. The OpenSciEd anchor
  from the standards connector is the first place to look, without naming the curriculum.
- **Students draw a first model early.** A quick labeled drawing of how they think it works, in a
  "Draw it" box on the packet, before the investigation.
- **The investigation produces evidence**: an observation, a measurement, a data table, a
  prediction checked against a chart. Where the data has numbers, students use the numbers.
- **One crosscutting concept per lesson**, named on a slide and used by students in a stem
  ("The pattern I see is ___", "___ caused ___ because ___", "The energy went from ___ to ___").
  It goes in their talk and in their writing, not only in the plan.
- **Students revise the model** on the same packet page, next to the first one: "Draw it again.
  Show how, not only what." A revised model shows the mechanism; a labeled picture of parts does
  not count.
- **The closing is a short claim-evidence-reasoning**, three stems: a claim, one piece of evidence
  *from today's investigation* with a number when the data has numbers, and reasoning that uses the
  crosscutting concept. A student who skipped the investigation should not be able to write it.

The lesson plan carries three labeled targets for a science lesson: **Practice** (what students
do, a science and engineering practice), **Core idea** (what they understand), and **Crosscutting
concept** (the lens they use). The student sees one "I can…" sentence on the slide.

## Reading level and load

Student text reads at 3rd-5th grade: short sentences, everyday words, one instruction at a time.
Grade 9-10 *content* in accessible *sentences* — never a watered-down idea in easy words. Say
"home language," and print a translation only into a language the profile names.

## Voice

Direct and warm, addressed to the student. Short sentences. No exclamation-point enthusiasm, no
cutesy framing, no emoji. Treat a 15-year-old like a 15-year-old: the reading is easier, the
respect isn't.
