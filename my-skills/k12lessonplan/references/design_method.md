# The design method this skill runs on

A lesson is a designed artifact for a known user in a known context, which is exactly the problem
the Stanford d.school's method was built for. This skill's steps *are* that method's modes, in
order — not as an homage, but because each mode fixes a specific way lesson-building goes wrong.

| Skill step | Mode | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Step 0-1, find the context | **Empathize** | Designing for a generic student instead of the people in this room |
| Step 2, first half | **Define** | Building activities before knowing what is actually hard about the idea |
| Step 2, second half | **Ideate** | Committing to the first workable idea because it was first |
| Step 3, build | **Prototype** | Polishing something nobody has looked at yet |
| Step 4, hand over and teach it | **Test** | Treating the deck as finished when it has never met a student |

---

## Define: the point of view

Before any activity exists, write one sentence naming who this is for and what is actually hard.
The d.school's shape is *[user] needs [need] because [insight]* — the insight being the part you
learned, not the part you assumed:

> A ninth grader who reads at a fourth-grade level needs to *see* surface area before naming it, because
> the words "surface" and "area" both sound like they mean the flat top.

That last clause is the whole lesson's centre of gravity. If the misconception in it came from the
Learning Commons graph (Step 1.5), better still — it is then documented rather than guessed.

A point of view that could be written about any class on any day is not a point of view. "Students
need practice with surface area because they haven't done it yet" names nothing and constrains
nothing.

## Define: how might we

Turn the point of view into one design question, phrased so several answers are possible:

> How might we make surface area something a student can hold in their hands?

It should be narrow enough to rule things out and wide enough that the first answer isn't the only
one. "How might we teach surface area" is too wide to help. "How might we get students to use the
formula 6s²" is a solution wearing a question mark.

## Ideate: flare, then focus

The reliable failure in lesson-building is *converging too early* — taking the first workable video,
the first plausible photo, the first context for the word problem, because each was good enough.
The move is to open before you close:

**Flare.** Generate more options than you need, deliberately, before judging any of them. Three
contexts for the math problem. Eight to twelve photo candidates per slot. Two or three videos.
Quantity first; judgement is a separate pass and mixing them kills the quantity.

**Focus.** Then converge against a criterion you wrote *before* you had options to be lenient
about — the frame test for a photograph, the point of view for an activity. A criterion written
afterwards is a justification for what you already picked.

This is why the presentation skill's photographs reference pulls 8-12 candidates and expects to reject.
Rejection is not waste; it is the only thing that makes the choice mean anything.

The talk-through in Step 2 is where the flare becomes visible to the teacher. Offering two real
videos with a recommendation is ideation made reviewable; offering one is a decision already made.

## Prototype: build to think

The deck and the packet are prototypes, not deliverables. They exist to be looked at, argued with,
and changed. Two consequences:

- **Rough beats polished, early.** The Step 2 talk-through is a prototype in text, and it is cheap
  precisely because nothing is rendered. Revisions there cost a sentence; the same change after the
  deck is built costs a rebuild. Never skip ahead to building because the plan seems obvious.
- **Build the least that makes the idea reviewable.** A slide that exists to show what a slide will
  be like is worth more than a paragraph describing it.

## Test: the deck meets students

A deck that has never been in front of a class is untested, however clean it checks. Testing is not
asking whether the teacher likes it; it is what happens in the room.

So the handover names **what to watch for**: the specific moment the lesson could fail, and the
observable that would tell the teacher it did. "If they can't say which faces are hidden, the box model
didn't land — that shows up at the third question, not the first."

And feedback comes back in the d.school's protocol, which separates reaction from redesign:

- **I like** — what worked, named specifically enough to keep.
- **I wish** — what didn't, stated as a want rather than a verdict.
- **What if** — the speculative fork, permitted to be impractical.

Use it when asking for the teacher's reaction, and use it on your own draft before they see it. The value is
that "I wish" is easier to say honestly than "this is wrong," so more real information moves.

## Show, don't tell — the one mindset that touches every slide

The rest of the method shapes the process; this one shapes each artifact, which is why it also
appears in the slideshow skill's deck reference as the test a slide has to pass.

The strongest version of any slide is the one where the evidence is on screen and the sentence is
unnecessary. Before writing a sentence, ask what could be shown instead: the photograph, the two
bars, the four steps. A sentence explaining a chart usually means the chart is doing too little.

Its companion is **beginner's mind**: you know what a greenhouse looks like, and the student does
not. Every place the lesson assumes a picture the student doesn't have is a place a photograph has
to go. That assumption is invisible from inside expertise, which is why it needs a rule rather than
a judgement call.
