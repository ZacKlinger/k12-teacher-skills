**Claude:** Here's the lesson. The file: *Beaks as tools - packet.docx*.

**Objective and standard.**
Big Idea: an animal's body parts have shapes that do specific jobs, and the shape is the reason the job gets done.
I can use evidence from a test to explain how the shape of a beak helps a bird get its food.
4-LS1-1: "Construct an argument that plants and animals have internal and external structures that function to support survival, growth, behavior, and reproduction." Targets: Practice, an argument from evidence with a model; Core idea, external structures support survival (LS1.A); Crosscutting concept, structure and function. (The standard's own crosscutting concept is systems; that comes in when the unit puts several parts together, the next move below.) Students already know: how to back a claim with something they observed about where animals survive (3-LS4-3: some organisms survive well in a habitat; best knowledge).

**Agenda.** Do Now 4 · Objective + Agenda 2 · Two birds, two beaks 5 · Test the tools 12 · Make sense of it 8 · Name it + draw it again 8 · Closing (CER) 6 = 45 minutes.

**The blocks.**
- *Do Now (4).* "Draw a bird eating. Label the beak and write what it's doing." This is their first model; don't correct it. Watch for drawings with a beak and no food, and ask "what is it eating?"
- *Two birds, two beaks (5).* Project the two photographs. Read the key-words note aloud. Say: "Both of these birds eat every day. Look at their beaks. Why so different?" Turn-and-talk, then take two guesses without judging them. Leave the question on the board.
- *Test the tools (12).* Model one trial in 60 seconds: tool in one hand, the other hand behind your back, partner says go and counts to 20, count what's in the cup, write it, switch. Students circle their fish prediction, then run nine trials. Look-fors:
  1. A student keeps their second hand behind their back on every trial. A two-handed trial measures the student, not the tool. Move: "would a bird have a second hand?"
  2. Partners write a number in every cell, zeros included. A zero is the strongest evidence in the table (a spoon with worms in a tall cup). Move: "is a zero a result?"
  3. A student turns a tool to beat the food, sideways tweezers as a scoop. A structure-function idea is arriving early. Move: "what shape did you just make it into?"

  The table has no single right answer: counts vary by group. The pattern is what to check.
- *Make sense of it (8).* Partners decide which tool won each food and why, then write question 3. Circulate for answers that name the winner without the shape.
- *Name it + draw it again (8).* Tally the class's winners on the board, three foods by three tools. Say: "The shape is the **structure**. The job is the **function**. You just showed that the structure decides the function." Then read the heron line and give students 5 minutes on question 4. Hold up one drawing with arrows that show the worm being pinched and lifted.
- *Closing (6).* Read the evidence key-word note. Students write the CER on their own, then question 6 with its stem. Collect.

**Student talk.** (1) Turn-and-talk on the photographs: "Why would these two birds have such different beaks?" Partner A (the one sitting closer to the board) starts, "I think the ___ beak is for ___ because ___"; Partner B says back what they heard, then adds their own. Thirty seconds of think time first. (2) Partner decide-and-write on question 3: "Which tool was best for each food, and what about its shape made it best?" Partner B starts this time, "The ___ was best for ___ because its shape ___"; Partner A points to the number in the table that backs it, or says "show me the number." They write what they decided. The Name it block opens from this talk: call two pairs whose answers name a shape, and end on the class sentence "the structure decides the function."

**Media.** No video: the test is the media. Two photographs, projected: a hummingbird feeding at a tubular flower, there to show a beak that reaches into a narrow space; a finch holding a seed in its beak, there to show a short, thick beak that cracks and grips. (Search Wikimedia Commons for "hummingbird feeding" and "finch eating seed"; the slide deck, if you want one, finds and credits them for you.)

**Differentiation.** No specific needs named, so the universal design defaults: stems on every written answer (questions 3 and 5), the first model drawn before anything is written, and every direction short enough to read alone (the page reads at about a 4th grade level or below). Stems also on question 6. UDL: engagement, every student commits to a prediction before the test; representation, the same idea as a tool, a photograph and a drawing; action and expression, students draw the model and say their reason to a partner before they write it.

**Predicted errors.**
1. Writes "the spoon was best for fish because it's a spoon." Why: they report the winner, and the tool's name feels like a reason. Move: "Point to the part of the spoon that did the work. What shape is it?"
2. Says "the heron's beak got long because it kept reaching into the mud." Why: everyday reasoning that using a part changes it; it's a common idea about how animals come to fit their food. Move: "Today we ask what the shape does, not how it got that way. What does a long beak do in mud?" (Where traits come from was Grade 3, and natural selection is middle school; don't argue it today.)
3. Redraws the heron as a labeled picture: beak, eye, legs, with no action. Why: they treat a model as a picture of parts. Move: "Add one arrow for what the beak does to the worm. Where does the worm go next?"
4. Writes evidence with no number: "the tweezers were good at worms." Why: the table felt like work, not evidence. Move: "How many in 20 seconds? That number is your evidence."

**Answer key.** (1) Any drawing with a beak, food, and a label for what the beak does. (2) Prediction: any. Expected pattern (counts vary): tweezers win worms in the tall cup; spoon wins the floating fish; clothespin or tweezers win seeds; the spoon moves few or no worms. (3) For example: "The spoon was best for fish because its shape is a scoop that holds water and food." (4) A long, thin beak reaching into mud, closing on a worm, with arrows for reach, pinch and lift. (5) Claim: a beak's shape helps a bird get a certain food. Evidence: a count from their own table, e.g. "the tweezers moved 6 worms in 20 seconds and the spoon moved 0." Reasoning: a long, thin structure reaches into narrow places and pinches, so its function is catching worms. (6) Any honest answer.

**Closing check.** A correct CER names a food in the claim, cites a count from the student's own table (a zero counts), and connects a shape (long, thin, scoop, short and strong) to a job. If half the class writes evidence with no number, or reasoning that says "because it's better," open tomorrow with two sample CERs on the board, one with a number and a shape and one without, and have partners say which is stronger and why, before the next lesson.

**If you have to cut.** Keep the test, question 3, and the CER: they are where students argue from evidence, which is the standard. Cut the heron redraw to a one-minute verbal "what would its beak be like," and keep the class tally.

**What to watch for.** The lesson turns at question 3: if pairs write which tool won but nothing about its shape, structure and function haven't landed, and the CER will come back with "because it's better." It shows there, not in the data table.

Materials per pair: tweezers, a clothespin, a plastic spoon, an empty cup. Per table: a plate of sunflower seeds, rubber-band pieces in a tall narrow cup, cereal floating in a bowl of water.
