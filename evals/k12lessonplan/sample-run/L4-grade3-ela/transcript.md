**Point of view:** Third graders who can retell a fable need to say what it teaches *people*, because when a story is a contest they report who won ("the Sun is stronger"), and that is the plot, not the moral.

**Objective and standard.**
Big Idea: a fable's lesson is never said by the characters; readers find it in what changes between key details.
I can find the moral of a fable and explain it with key details from the story.
RL.3.2: "Recount stories, including fables, folktales, and myths from diverse cultures; determine the central message, lesson, or moral and explain how it is conveyed through key details in the text." Students already know: how to retell a fable and name its lesson when the lesson is fairly plain (RL.2.2: recount fables and name their moral); check that they can retell beginning, middle and end.

**The text.** *The Wind and the Sun*, retold from Aesop (public domain), 4 paragraphs, about 150 words; *The Crow and the Water Jug*, retold, 4 sentences. Complexity: [suggested] about 550-650L, an estimate, inside the grade 2-3 band (420-820L): short sentences, everyday words, dialogue. The demand is qualitative: Aesop's moral line is left off the page, so students determine it from the details, which is the standard.

**Agenda.** Do Now 5 · Objective + Agenda 2 · Read 15 · Write first, then talk 10 · Discuss 10 · Closing 8 = 50 minutes.

**The blocks.**
- *Do Now (5).* Question 1, on their own. Take two answers aloud: listen for a lesson said as advice ("be kind"), and name it: "that's a lesson for people."
- *Read (15).* Read the key-words note aloud. Read the fable aloud once; students follow with a finger under the words. Then reread paragraph 3 together and think aloud: "The Wind blew a cold blast. What did the man do? He pulled his coat closer. That goes in the Wind column, and so does what the man did." Students finish the chart from paragraphs 3 and 4 with a partner nearby. Look-fors:
  1. A student writes what the man did in each column, not only the Wind's and the Sun's actions. The man's change is the key detail the moral rests on. Move: "And then what did the man do?"
  2. A student points to a paragraph number when asked where a detail is. A detail they can find is evidence; one they remember is a guess. Move: "Which paragraph? Put your finger on it."
  3. A student writes "the Sun won" in the Sun column. That is the plot creeping in as a detail. Move: "Show me the words that tell what the Sun did."
- *Write first, then talk (10).* Read the moral note aloud. Everyone writes question 3 alone (4 minutes), then write-pair-share. Several wordings of the moral are right: "being gentle works better than force," "you can't make people do things by pushing," "kindness gets more done than being rough." Mark the idea, not the words.
- *Discuss (10).* Read Sam's sentence aloud. Students write question 4 first (3 minutes), then agree or disagree with a partner, then share. The one idea it has to reach: a moral is a lesson for people, and the details show it (the harder the Wind blew, the tighter the coat; the gentle Sun got it off). End by writing the class's moral on the board, and only then say Aesop's: "Persuasion is better than force," in kid words, "being kind works better than pushing."
- *Closing (8).* Read the crow fable aloud once. Students write question 5 and question 6 on their own. Collect.

**Student talk.** (1) Write-pair-share on question 3, after writing: Partner A (the one sitting closer to the door) reads their moral; Partner B says it back, "You said…", and names one detail from the chart that fits it; then switch. Stem: "My moral is ___. A detail that shows it is ___." (2) Agree or disagree with Sam on question 4, after writing: Partner B goes first this time and reads their answer; Partner A agrees, disagrees, or adds on, with a paragraph number. Stems: "I agree with Sam because…", "I disagree with Sam because…", "I want to add…" The whole-class share opens with two pairs who disagree with each other.

**Media.** No video and no photographs: the read-aloud is the media, and the chart is the picture of the story's turn.

**Differentiation.** Multilingual learners, Arabic: every question, every direction, the key-word notes and the headings carry one short Arabic line, printed right to left; the story itself stays in English and is read aloud first. Students reading below grade level: the story stays at grade level, with a read-aloud first, numbered paragraphs for finding details, the close read limited to two paragraphs, and directions written short (outside the story, the page reads at about a 1st-2nd grade level). Stems on every composed answer (questions 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6). UDL: engagement, the do-now starts from a story in their own life; representation, the story heard and read, the key words defined where they land in English and Arabic, and the turn drawn as a two-column chart; action and expression, the T-chart organizes the evidence, and every answer is written before it is said to a partner.

**Predicted errors.**
1. Writes the moral as "The Sun is stronger than the Wind." Why: the story opens as a contest, so "who won" feels like the point. Move: "Is that a lesson for a person? What should *you* do, from this story?"
2. Writes "Never give up," because the Wind kept blowing harder. Why: a familiar moral from other stories fits loosely, and they reach for a stock lesson. Move: "Did trying harder work for the Wind? Which detail shows what *did* work?"
3. Names a detail that doesn't change: "The man had a long coat." Why: any detail feels like a key detail. Move: "Does that detail change when the Sun comes out? Find one that changes."
4. Retells the whole crow fable for question 5 instead of naming a moral. Why: retelling is the skill they already own. Move: "In one sentence: what should a person learn from the crow?"

**Answer key.** (1) Any story and lesson. (2) Wind: blew a cold blast, blew harder and harder, leaves flew; the man pulled his coat closer, held it shut with both hands, bent his head. Sun: shone warm and bright, kept shining gently; the man opened his coat, took it off, sat down to rest. (3) A lesson for people that the details support: being gentle or kind works better than force; several wordings are right. (4) Disagree: that is what happened, not a lesson for people. Detail: the harder the Wind blew, the tighter the man held his coat (paragraph 3), but when the Sun shone gently, he took it off (paragraph 4). (5) A lesson such as "little by little, a hard job gets done" or "keep trying in a smart way," shown by "she dropped in one pebble, then another … little by little, the water rose." (6) Any honest answer.

**Closing check.** A correct exit names a moral as a lesson for people and backs it with a detail from the crow fable (the pebbles, one at a time). If half the class writes plot ("the crow drank the water"), open tomorrow with six sentence cards to sort, moral or plot, before the next fable.

**If you have to cut.** Keep the chart (question 2), Sam (question 4) and the crow (question 5): the chart is the evidence, Sam is the error, the crow is the test. Take the do-now aloud and fold question 3 into the Sam discussion.

**What to watch for.** The lesson turns at question 3: if students write "the Sun won" or "the Sun is stronger," they are reporting the plot, not the moral. Question 4 is built to catch it, but you'll see it first at question 3.
