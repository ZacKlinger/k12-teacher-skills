**Objective and standard.**
Big Idea: a proportional relationship keeps one ratio everywhere, including at zero.
I can decide if a table shows a proportional relationship by checking the ratio in every row.
7.RP.A.2a: "Decide whether two quantities are in a proportional relationship, e.g., by testing for equivalent ratios in a table or graphing on a coordinate plane and observing whether the graph is a straight line through the origin." Students already know: equivalent ratios in a ratio table (6.RP.A.3a; best knowledge, the connector returned no prerequisite).

**Agenda.** Do Now 5 · Objective + Agenda 2 · Which one? 10 · Partner test 15 · Discuss 10 · Closing 8 = 50 minutes.

**The blocks.**
- *Do Now (5).* "Finish the flour table on your own." Watch for students adding 2 each row without checking the batches column; ask "how many batches is that row?"
- *Which one? (10).* Two word slides first, proportional and ratio, matching the key-words note above question 2. Then the two tables. Say: "Both go up by the same amount every row. Vote: which is proportional?" Count hands, run the turn-and-talk, count again. Model the test on the snow cones, one row at a time ("3.00 divided by 2 is 1.50; now the next row"), reveal the unit-rate chart with a guess before each bar, and play the video's first table only (about 90 seconds). Students fill question 3.
- *Partner test (15).* Faucet first (question 4), then partner compare, then the engraving shop (question 5). Both tests are worked in the box, with the sentence written under the work. Look-fors:
  1. A student writes liters ÷ hours for every row, not just the first. One equal row proves nothing. Move: "show me row 3."
  2. A student calls the engraving table proportional "because it goes up by 2." That's today's trap. Move: "divide row 1 and row 4; same answer?"
  3. A student finds 1.5 for the faucet but writes "not proportional" because the liters aren't whole numbers. Move: "is 1.5 the same in every row?"

  Question 5(b) has one right table (1 letter $2, 2 letters $4, 4 letters $8); a student who writes it as a rule, "$2 times the letters," is also right.
- *Discuss (10).* Opens with Ana's claim, the engraving graph, and 45 seconds of think time; partners talk, then write question 6. The one idea it has to reach: adding the same amount is not the test; the same ratio is, and a starting fee shows up as a line that crosses the cost axis at $6 instead of (0, 0). Ends with two pairs called by the picker and the checkpoint slide.
- *Closing (8).* Exit question 7 on their own, then the reflection line. Collect at the door.

**Student talk.** (1) Vote, talk, revote on question 2: Partner A gives a vote and a reason, Partner B says it back and adds theirs; stem "I voted ___ because…". (2) Partner compare after question 4: partners point to any row where their unit rates differ; A explains a row, B says it back and explains another; stem "In this row, liters ÷ hours is ___, so…". (3) Agree / disagree / add on to Ana's claim in Discuss: three stems on the slide; the picker calls two pairs after they've rehearsed. Partner A is the student nearer the window.

**Media.** Khan Academy, "Identifying a proportional relationship from a table" (runtime to confirm; stop after the first table). Purpose before it plays: "count how many rows he checks." No photographs this lesson. The unit-rate bar chart and the engraving graph carry the visuals.

**Differentiation.** Sentence stems on every written answer (questions 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8). Word bank: the key-words note above question 3. Chunked directions: one instruction per line on every slide. Visual support: every question has its slide with the table drawn as cards or a chart. Language access: questions 1-8 and the key-words note carry Spanish and Vietnamese lines on the packet and the slides; headings carry none. UDL: engagement, the vote puts every student's guess in play; representation, the same test as a table, a bar chart and a graph; action and expression, every written answer is said to a partner first.

**Predicted errors.**
1. Calls bowling proportional: "it goes up $4 every row." Why: they test the change between rows, not the ratio in each row. Move: "What does one game cost in row 1? In row 3?"
2. Writes the engraving unit rate as 2 for every row (the change), not 8, 5, 3.5. Why: same confusion, written as a number. Move: "Divide the cost by the letters in row 1. Is it 2?"
3. Writes the faucet rate as 0.67 (hours ÷ liters) in one row and 1.5 in another. Why: dividing in a different order in different rows. Move: "Pick one order and keep it; liters first, every row."

**Answer key.** (1) 6 batches: 4 cups; 9: 6; 12: 8. (2) Snow cones. (3) Snow cones 1.50, 1.50, 1.50: yes. Bowling 7.00, 5.50, 5.00: no. (4) 1.5 in every row: proportional, 1.5 liters an hour. (5a) 8, 5, 3.5: not proportional; the $6 setup fee. (5b) 1 letter $2, 2 letters $4, 4 letters $8: proportional, $2 a letter. (6) Disagree: the same amount added each row isn't the test; the ratio changes (8, 5, 3.5). At 0 letters the cost would be $6, so the graph crosses the cost axis at 6 and does not go through (0, 0). (7) 5, 4, 3.67: not proportional. (8) Any honest answer.

**Closing check.** A correct exit answer divides all three rows (5, 4, 3.67), says they differ, and names the starting fee or says the ratio isn't the same. If half the class misses it, open tomorrow with a three-table sort (proportional, steady but not proportional, neither) before any new content.

**If you have to cut.** Keep the engraving question, the Discuss block and the closing: they are the hardest case and the only places students argue with the misconception. Cut the video and question 3's bowling row.
