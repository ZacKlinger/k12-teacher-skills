**Claude:** Here's Monday's lesson. The files: *Science 1.7 - Pump test - packet.docx* and *Science 1.7 - Pump test - deck.html* (open it in Chrome). Built from Science 1.6.

**Objective and standard.**
Big Idea: a system works only when every part does its job where it's needed; engineers check a part with a number, at the place the criteria name, and every fix costs something.
I can test our pump at two heights and use the flow rate to judge it against our criteria.
HS-ETS1-3: "Evaluate a solution to a complex real-world problem based on prioritized criteria and trade-offs that account for a range of constraints, including cost, safety, reliability, and aesthetics as well as possible social, cultural, and environmental impacts." Targets: Practice, carrying out a test and judging a design against criteria; Core idea, choosing a fix means weighing constraints such as cost, noise and space (ETS1.B); Crosscutting concept, systems and system models. Students already know: criteria and constraint, and what the pump needs to work (Science 1.6, HS-ETS1-1); the system broken into six parts (Science 1.5, HS-ETS1-2); dividing to find a rate with a worked example in front of them (6.RP.A.3b; best knowledge).

**Agenda.** Breakfast + Do Now 10 · Objective + Agenda 3 · Will it reach the top? 10 · Build and test 20 · Stretch 3 · Make sense of it 12 · Name it + watch 15 · Draw it again 10 · Closing 10 = 93 minutes.

**The blocks.**
- *Breakfast + Do Now (10).* Students eat and answer question 1. Say: "Last Monday you named one thing that could go wrong. Today we find out if it does." Watch for "it breaks" with nothing to see; ask "what would we see?"
- *Will it reach the top? (10).* Two word slides (criteria, then system), the vote with the diagram, then three minutes on the first model. Say: "The pump sits here, on the bottom. The top channel is 120 cm up. Same pump. More water up there, the same, or less?" Count round 1, run the turn-and-talk, count round 2. Don't say who's right.
- *Build and test (20).* Before class: tote filled to the line, pump and tubing out, the 1-liter bottle marked, the safety outlet tested. Click through the four build steps one at a time, then teach the tool, not the answer: the flow-rate slide's worked example, and one practice timing at the bottom channel together. Then pairs time both channels. Click each pair's numbers into the bottom plot as they report, then ask "where will the top dots land?" before filling the second. Look-fors:
  1. A pair holds the tube's end at the channel's real height, not a convenient one. Height is the whole variable. Move: "is that where the water will come out?"
  2. A student writes 60 ÷ seconds, not seconds ÷ 60. 0.67 reads as "fails" when the pump is fine. Move: point to the worked example: "which number goes first?"
  3. A student says the top is slower before the dots fill in, with a reason about lifting. The mechanism is arriving from the evidence. Move: "hold that; say it in the talk."

  Flow rates differ pair to pair; the pattern, not a single number, is the answer.
- *Stretch (3).* Everyone walks to the frame and looks up at the top channel.
- *Make sense of it (12).* Partner compare on question 5 (8 minutes, phased), then question 6 on their own (4).
- *Name it + watch (15).* Click through the Name it slide: "The pump gives the same push every second. Lifting water 120 cm uses part of that push. So less comes out at the top. One part of the system, height, changed what another part, the pump, could do." Then the two-fixes slide: "If the top falls short, we can add push or take away lift. Each one costs us something." Then the prime word slide, and the video with its purpose first: "watch how they prime the pump, and what they do first." Stop after the pump is primed (time to confirm when you preview it).
- *Draw it again (10).* Students keep their first model and add arrow thickness and their two numbers. Hold up one drawing with a thin arrow labeled with its flow rate.
- *Closing (10).* The CER on their own, with the word bank: which fix, and the top-channel number behind it. Then question 10. Collect.

**Student talk.** (1) Vote, talk, revote on question 2: Partner A (holds the tube today) gives a guess and a reason; Partner B says it back, "You said…", then adds theirs; stem "I think so because ___"; the picker calls one pair after the revote. (2) Partner compare on question 5: both find their two numbers; Partner A says both numbers and a reason; Partner B (ran the timer) says it back, then agrees or adds a reason; stem "I think they are different because ___"; both write; the picked pair reports. The Discuss moment is the share: it opens with the reporting pair, the one idea it has to reach is that height takes part of the pump's push, and it ends on the Name it slide.

**Predicted errors.**
1. Circles "Yes" on question 6 using the bottom-channel number (4 liters a minute). Why: a pump that splashes water reads as "working," and the bottom number is the bigger one. Move: "Read criterion 2 out loud. Where does it say to measure?"
2. Writes the flow rate as seconds ÷ 60: 40 ÷ 60 = 0.67, then calls the pump a failure. Why: dividing the bigger-looking number into the smaller is a habit, and 0.67 is minutes per liter. Move: "It filled a liter in less than a minute. Is the flow more than 1 liter a minute, or less?"
3. Says the top gets less "because the tube is longer." Why: the longer tube is the difference they can see; the height is the one they can't. Move: "Keep the same tube, but hold the end low. Same length; what happens to the flow?"
4. Redraws the model as a labeled picture with the same arrows as before. Why: they treat a model as a picture of parts. Move: "Which arrow carries more water? Make it thicker and write its number on it."

**Answer key.** (1) Any risk from 1.6 with something we'd see: a leak (water on the floor), no flow (nothing at the top), a loud pump. (2) Prediction: any; the test shows less. (3) First model: any drawing of tote, pump, tubing and channels. (4) Numbers vary by pump; for example, bottom 15 seconds = 4 liters a minute, top 40 seconds = 1.5 liters a minute. The top is always slower. (5) The top got less because the pump uses part of its push to lift the water higher. (6) Yes if the top-channel flow is at least 1 liter a minute; the reason uses the top number. (7) They prime it: the pump and line are filled with water and the air is pushed out before it runs; confirm against the video. (8) A thick arrow to the bottom channel and a thin one to the top, each labeled with its flow rate, and a note that height uses part of the push. (9) Claim: either fix, with what it costs (more money and noise, or fewer plants); evidence: the top-channel flow rate from question 4; reasoning: when the water has to go higher, the pump moves less, so a stronger pump adds push or a lower channel takes away lift. Both fixes are right answers; the reason is what's graded. (10) Any honest answer.

**Closing check.** A correct CER uses the top-channel number, not the bottom one, says the height took part of the pump's push, and names what the chosen fix costs. If half the class uses the bottom number, open Wednesday with two claims side by side, one using each number, and have partners vote which one tests our criteria and why, before planning tubing.
